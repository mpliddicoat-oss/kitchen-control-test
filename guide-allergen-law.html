UK Allergen Law: What It Actually Requires Of Your Kitchen

Not Natasha's Law — that's for pre-packaged food. This is the law that governs every UK restaurant, cafe and pub serving food to a customer, whether it's plated, packaged, or handed over the counter.

Kitchen Control · Guides

The law, in plain terms

The Food Information Regulations 2014 (FIR) enforce the retained EU rules on food information — commonly referred to as the "FIC" — which require every UK food business to declare any of 14 major allergens present in the food they serve. This applies to non-prepacked food (a meal cooked and served to a table), prepacked food, and food packaged on-site for direct sale. In practice: if you cook it and someone eats it, this law applies to you.

It came into force in December 2014 and it's not optional guidance — it's enforced by your local authority, usually via Environmental Health, and non-compliance can lead to an improvement notice and, in some cases, prosecution.

Does this apply to you?
✓You cook or prepare food that a customer eats on-site or takes away
✓You run a restaurant, cafe, pub, takeaway, or catering business
✓You serve food at events, pop-ups, or markets

If any of these are true, this law applies to you today — regardless of whether you package or pre-wrap anything.

The 14 allergens

These must be declared whenever present as an ingredient, including in additives, processing aids, and anything used in a stock, sauce, or garnish — not just the obvious main ingredient.

How you're allowed to provide it

The FSA gives you three acceptable routes, and you can choose whichever fits how your kitchen runs:

1. Written up front — allergens marked directly on the menu or menu board, so a customer sees the information without asking.

2. Signposted to written information — a note on the menu pointing to an allergen matrix or folder available on request.

3. Signposted to verbal information — a note telling the customer to ask a member of staff, provided that staff member gives accurate, consistent answers backed by something written.

Where kitchens get caught out Verbal-only answers are technically allowed, but the FSA is explicit that they need to be backed up by accurate written information behind the scenes — not memory. The most common compliance failure isn't a missing menu note, it's a staff member giving a confident, wrong answer because the allergen data lived in someone's head rather than somewhere checkable. A supplier changing an ingredient without anyone updating the sheet is the other repeat offender.

What good practice actually looks like

In practice, most independent kitchens land on a combination: allergens marked or flagged on the menu for common ones, plus a written allergen matrix — a simple grid of dishes against the 14 allergens — kept up to date and available the moment a customer or an EHO asks for it. The matrix only stays useful if it's rebuilt every time a recipe or supplier changes, which is where most manual, spreadsheet-based versions quietly go out of date.
